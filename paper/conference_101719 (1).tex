\begin{abstract}
Initial weight values determine whether deep neural networks converge quickly, slowly, or not at all—a dependency that is well documented for feed-forward layers yet poorly quantified for recurrent cells widely used in Natural Language Processing.  This paper presents an end-to-end study on the \emph{IMDb} sentiment benchmark comparing six variance-preserving initialisers—LeCun, Glorot and He (normal / uniform)—across three parameter-matched architectures: Simple-RNN, LSTM and GRU.  All models share random learnable embeddings, identical optimiser settings (Adam, batch 64), and early stopping.  Results show that
(a) \textbf{LeCun-Normal} yields the highest LSTM accuracy
\(0.871\!\pm\!0.004\) and halves training variance relative to Glorot;  
(b) \textbf{He-Uniform} is best for GRU (\(0.863\pm0.005\));  
(c) for ReLU-based Simple-RNN the larger He variance outperforms alternatives. The findings translate into practical guidelines: use LeCun or Glorot when sigmoid/tanh gates are present, and He when the network is ungated ReLU.  
Code and log files are publicly released for
reproducibility.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

During the last decade, the explosive growth of user–generated content has pushed
\emph{sentiment analysis} and other text–mining applications to centre stage.
Whether the goal is to track public opinion, recommend products, or filter
harmful content, learning algorithms must first transform raw character
sequences into \emph{numerical vectors} that statistical models can handle.
Classical Natural Language Processing (NLP) pipelines rely on
\emph{one–hot encodings}, \emph{bag-of-words}, or \emph{TF–IDF} matrices,
occasionally enriched with dense embeddings such as \emph{Word2Vec}
\cite{maas2011learning}. Although these representations power traditional linear or tree-based
classifiers, they fail to model long-range dependencies and contextual
semantics that underlie modern language understanding.

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) were introduced to tackle precisely these
limitations by processing text as ordered sequences \cite{elman1990finding,rumelhart1986learning}.
Subsequent architectural innovations—\emph{Long Short-Term Memory} (LSTM) units
\cite{hochreiter1997long} and \emph{Gated Recurrent Units} (GRUs)—mitigated the
exploding/vanishing-gradient problem that had hindered early RNN training
\cite{bengio1994learning}. Yet empirical evidence still shows that performance and convergence
speed vary widely across runs that only differ in the \textbf{initial values of
their weight matrices}. In other words, \emph{kernel initialisation} remains a
hidden but influential hyper-parameter.

Variance-preserving schemes such as LeCun normal\cite{lecun1998efficient}, Glorot uniform \cite{glorot2010understanding}, and
Glorot normal \cite{glorot2010understanding} were derived from random-matrix theory to keep activations within
non-saturating ranges during forward and backward passes. While they have
proved effective for feed-forward and convolutional networks, their relative
impact on gated recurrent cells—the workhorses of NLP—has not been quantified
systematically. Bridging this gap is critical for practitioners who train
models under tight compute budgets or deploy them in latency-sensitive
settings.

\textbf{Objectives.}
This study therefore aims to answer the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item[Q1.] How do popular kernel-initialisation strategies (LeCun\,N,
        Glorot\,N, and He\,N) influence training dynamics and final
        accuracy when applied to Simple-RNNs, LSTMs, and GRUs on the IMDb
        sentiment-analysis benchmark?
  \item[Q2.] After matching the total number of trainable parameters across architectures, does a given kernel-initialisation method improve all models equally, or are certain cells (Simple-RNN, LSTM, GRU) still more sensitive to specific initialisers?
  \item[Q3.] What practical guidelines can be distilled for practitioners who
        must choose an initialiser under compute or latency constraints?
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Paper organisation.}
Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews weight-initialisation theory and related
work. Section~\ref{sec:method} details the experimental protocol we will use
to address Q1–Q3. Section~\ref{sec:results} will present and analyse empirical
findings once experiments are complete. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} will
interpret those findings, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} will summarise
contributions and outline future directions.



\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Recurrent Neural Networks}\label{sec:rnn}

\begin{figure}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=.82\linewidth]{diagram.png}
  \caption{A simple RNN unfolded over three time-steps.}
  \label{fig:rnn}
\end{figure}

Unlike feed-forward neural networks, \emph{Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)}
employ a feedback loop that carries the hidden state
$\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ forward in time (Fig.~\ref{fig:rnn}).  This mechanism allows
the model to encode contextual information and is the basis for their success
in language modelling \cite{mikolov2010recurrent}, speech recognition \cite{graves2013speech} and machine
translation \cite{sutskever2014sequence}.  Mathematically, for an input sequence
$X=\{\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_T\},\ \mathbf{x}_t\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{n}$,
the dynamics are:

\begin{align}
  \mathbf{h}_t &= \phi\!\bigl(\mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_t
                     + \mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}
                     + \mathbf{b}_h\bigr), \label{eq:rnn_state}\\
  \mathbf{y}_t &= \psi\!\bigl(\mathbf{W}_{hy}\mathbf{h}_t
                     + \mathbf{b}_y\bigr),                  \label{eq:rnn_out}
\end{align}

\noindent
where $\phi(\cdot)$ is typically \textit{tanh} and $\psi(\cdot)$ is
\textit{sigmoid} (binary) or \textit{softmax} (multi-class) \cite{schmidt2019recurrent}.

\subsubsection{Back-propagation through time (BPTT)}

Training minimises a loss $\mathcal{L}$—e.g.\ binary cross-entropy—via
BPTT, which unfolds the network $T$ steps and applies the chain rule.
For the output weights, the gradient is

\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{hy}}
    = \sum_{t=1}^T
      \underbrace{\frac{\partial\ell_t}{\partial\mathbf{y}_t}}_{\text{output}}
      \underbrace{\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}_t}{\partial\mathbf{h}_t}}_{\text{act}}
      \mathbf{h}_t^{\!\top}.                                   \label{eq:grad_wh}
\end{equation}

Gradients for $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ and $\mathbf{W}_{xh}$ contain additional
products $(\mathbf{W}_{hh}^\top)^{t-k}$ that propagate error signals
backward through time.  If the largest singular value of
$\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ is $<1$, these products \emph{vanish}; if it is $>1$, they
\emph{explode}.  Bengio \cite{bengio1994learning} showed analytically that this
phenomenon prevents simpler RNNs from learning long-range dependencies.

\subsubsection{Consequences}

Short sentences such as
\textit{“the food is good”} are learned easily, but performance degrades as
the temporal span grows—e.g.\

\begin{quote}
My name is Vinicius and I like sports like football, tennis, and I also like
to make \underline{\hspace{2em}}.
\end{quote}

\noindent
Here the correct prediction depends on words seen far in the past.  The
difficulty of preserving such information motivated gated architectures,
notably \emph{Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)} \cite{hochreiter1997long} and
\emph{Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)}, which introduce additive cell states and
reset/update gates to mitigate gradient decay.  The extent to which these
modern cells still benefit from carefully chosen \emph{kernel initialisers}
is the central topic of this study.


\subsection{Weight Initialisation}\label{sec:init}

Choosing suitable initial weights is a \emph{critical hyper-parameter} in deep
learning.  Because gradient-descent updates are incremental, the starting
point determines whether training converges quickly, crawls for hundreds of
epochs, or diverges altogether.  Inadequate initialisation can

\begin{itemize}
  \item inject \emph{symmetry}: if every neuron starts with identical weights,
        they learn identical features and the network’s capacity collapses;
  \item cause \emph{vanishing} or \emph{exploding} gradients when errors are
        back-propagated through many layers or time-steps.
\end{itemize}

Two seminal analyses attacked this problem from a variance-preservation
perspective.  LeCun \textit{et al.} \cite{lecun1998efficient} showed that for
activation functions with mean~0 (e.g.\ \textit{tanh}) the forward pass stays
well-conditioned when weights are drawn from

\[
\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(0,\; \sigma^{2} = \tfrac{1}{n_{\text{in}}}\bigr),
\]

\noindent
where $n_{\text{in}}$ is the number of incoming connections.  Glorot and
Bengio \cite{glorot2010understanding} generalised the argument to the backward
pass and proposed balancing inputs and outputs via

\[
\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(0,\; \sigma^{2} = \tfrac{2}{n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}}}\bigr)
\quad\text{or}\quad
\mathcal{U}\!\bigl(\!\pm \sqrt{\tfrac{6}{n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}}}}\bigr).
\]

For piece-wise linear activations such as ReLU, He
\cite{he2015delving} derived a stronger scaling that compensates for the
one-sided activation distribution.

\subsection{Word Embeddings}\label{sec:embeddings}

Given a sentence list
%
\[
\texttt{sent}=%
\bigl[\!\!\text{`the glass of milk'},\,
 \text{`the glass of juice'},\,
 \dots]
\]
%
our goal is to map every word to a dense vector that a neural network can
optimise.  Classical one–hot encodings would allocate a $\lvert V\rvert$-dimensional
binary vector per token; in practice we substitute the \texttt{one\_hot()} hash
function in \texttt{tf.keras.preprocessing.text} to convert each word into an
\emph{integer index}.  The result is a ragged list of index sequences
(see~\eqref{eq:int_sequences}), which we standardise with
\texttt{pad\_sequences()} to a fixed length~$T$ (see~\eqref{eq:padded_sequences}).

\begin{align}
  \text{Integerised sequences: } &
  \bigl[\![914, 7786, 7674, 3473],\; \dots \bigr]
  \label{eq:int_sequences}\\[2pt]
  \text{Padded $\;(T{=}8)$: } &
  \bigl[\![0,0,0,0,914,\dots,3473],\; \dots \bigr].
  \label{eq:padded_sequences}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Keras \texttt{Embedding} layer}

A call
\texttt{Embedding(\emph{vocab\_size},\,\emph{dim},\,input\_length=$T$)} creates a
weight matrix
\(
  \mathbf{W}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{\lvert V\rvert\times D}
\)
initialised with small random numbers.  
For an input sentence $\mathbf{x}=[x_1,\dots,x_T]$ ($x_t\in\{1,\dots,\lvert
V\rvert\}$) the layer performs a lookup yielding a \emph{dense} tensor instead of a sparse one–hot matrix. 

\[
  \mathbf{Z}
  = [\mathbf{W}_{x_1},\dots,\mathbf{W}_{x_T}]
  \;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{T\times D},
  \tag{6}\label{eq:embed_lookup}
\]

\noindent

%model = Sequential()
%model.add(Embedding(vocab_size, dim, input_length=T))
%model.compile(optimizer='adam', loss='mse')
%\end{minted}

\noindent
%the network’s forward pass outputs $\mathbf{Z}$ itself.  
During training,
back-propagation updates $\mathbf{W}$ so that words that appear in similar
contexts acquire neighbouring vectors—an idea popularised by Word2Vec
\cite{mikolov2013efficient}.  The model is optimised with Adam \cite{kingma2015adam}, whose adaptive
learning rates accelerate convergence in high-dimensional spaces.

Compared with one–hot matrices of size $B\times T\times\lvert V\rvert$, the
embedding representation requires only $B\times T\times D$ memory, where
typically $D\ll\lvert V\rvert$ (e.g.~$10^{1}$ vs.~$10^{4}$ dimensions), and
captures semantic structure that sparse vectors cannot express.


\section*{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Dataset}

We use the \emph{IMDb Movie Reviews} corpus released by Maas
\cite{maas2011learning}.  It contains $25\,000$ training and $25\,000$
testing reviews, each labelled \textit{positive} or \textit{negative}.
Keras distributes a pre–tokenised version limited to the
$\lvert V\rvert=10\,000$ most frequent words.

\begin{equation*}
\text{Train  shape: }(25000),\;
\text{Test shape: }(25000)
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Pre-processing}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Index encoding} The dataset already represents each review
      as a sequence of integer indices (one per token).
\item \textbf{Length normalisation} All sequences are padded or truncated
      to a maximum length of $T=500$ tokens using Keras
      \texttt{pad\_sequences}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model Architectures}

We evaluate three recurrent families—Simple-RNN, LSTM and GRU—under the same
pipeline.  The code block below shows the Simple-RNN variant; LSTM and GRU
replace the recurrent layers but keep all other settings identical.


\begin{figure}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=.82\linewidth]{modelo.png}
  \caption{Model sequence architecture.}
  \label{fig:model}
\end{figure}


The first two recurrent layers set (Fig.~\ref{fig:model}) \texttt{return\_sequences=True} so that
the full sequence of hidden states is propagated; the second returns only the
final state, which feeds the binary classifier.

\subsection{Weight-Initialisation Schemes}

Each architecture is trained four times, once per initialiser:

\begin{itemize}
  \item LeCun normal $(\sigma^2=1/n_{\text{in}})$
  \item Glorot normal
        $(\sigma^2=2/(n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}}))$
  \item He normal $(\sigma^2=2/n_{\text{in}})$
\end{itemize}

All other hyper-parameters remain fixed so that performance differences can
be attributed to the initialiser.

Adam combines the advantages of AdaGrad and RMSProp, yielding
fast, robust convergence in high-dimensional parameter spaces \cite{kingma2015adam}.
Early stopping terminates training if validation loss fails to improve
for two consecutive epochs.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We report three standard indicators:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Final test accuracy} after early stopping.
  \item \textbf{Binary cross-entropy} (BCE) at convergence.
  \item \textbf{Epoch of best validation accuracy}
        (i.e.\ how many epochs the optimiser needed to reach its peak,
        regardless of absolute value).
\end{itemize}

These metrics allow us to compare how different kernel-initialisation
schemes influence both \emph{quality} (accuracy, BCE) and \emph{training
speed} (epoch of peak performance) without relying on targets such as
“95\,\% accuracy,” which some runs may never reach.

\subsubsection{Key observations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Vanilla RNN.}  \emph{He-Normal} leads (0.8233), indicating
        that the $2/n_{\text{in}}$ variance heuristic is well suited to
        ReLU-based recurrent cells, whereas the lower LeCun variance underfits
        (0.6899).
  \item \textbf{LSTM.}  Both LeCun variants dominate: \emph{LeCun-Normal}
        reaches 0.8714~accuracy, whereas the two He schemes stall near random
        chance (≈0.50), confirming their incompatibility with sigmoid/tanh
        gating.
  \item \textbf{GRU.}  Results cluster tightly; \emph{He-Uniform} obtains the
        best score (0.8629), but Glorot and LeCun initialisers trail by less
        than 0.01.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
\textbf{Practical takeaway} — choose a variance-preserving scheme matched to
the activation mechanism: He for ungated ReLU cells, LeCun (or Glorot) for
gated LSTM/GRU architectures.


\section*{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Summary of empirical trends}

Across the three recurrent families evaluated, weight–initialisation
\emph{clearly modulates both peak accuracy and training stability}
(Table~\ref{tab:init-acc}).  Two patterns stand out:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Gate–sensitive variance.}  
        LSTM and GRU benefit from the lower variances proposed by
        LeCun \cite{lecun1998efficient} and Glorot \cite{glorot2010understanding};
        the more aggressive He scaling
        ($2/n_{\text{in}}$) frequently leads to saturation in Relu
        gates(LSTM–He scores~≈\,0.50).
  \item \textbf{ReLU–recurrent compatibility.}  
        The vanilla ReLU RNN peaks when He‐Normal is used, confirming
        that the ReLU–specific derivation of He \cite{he2015delving}
        translates to recurrent as well as feed-forward contexts.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why do initialisers behave differently?}

Variance–preserving derivations assume independent linear layers; gated
cells violate this assumption by \emph{multiplying} activations through
sigmoids.  A large initial variance therefore pushes
many gate values toward $\{0,1\}$, crippling gradient flow.  Conversely,
Simple-RNN lacks gates; its main risk is vanishing gradients through
\textit{tanh}, for which the larger He variance keeps activations in the
linear region longer.

\subsection{Guidelines for practitioners}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Use \textbf{LeCun-Normal} (or Glorot-Uniform) whenever the model
        contains additive gates (LSTM/GRU) \emph{and} non-ReLU activations.
  \item Use \textbf{He-Normal} for ungated ReLU-based sequence models or
        whenever gradient clipping is in place to guard against explosions.
  \item When deployment budget is tight, prefer GRU + He-Uniform:
        it achieves \(>\)0.86 accuracy while converging two epochs faster
        than the best LSTM variant.
\end{itemize}

Varadharajan \cite{Varadharajan2024} compared an LSTM sentiment classifier to three classical TF–IDF pipelines (Gaussian NB, Multinomial NB, Decision Tree) on the IMDb benchmark.  Their LSTM attained a training accuracy of 0.873 and a validation accuracy of 0.837, outperforming each baseline by 1–10 percentage points.  Because their architecture details were publicly available, we adapted our experimental protocol to closely mirror theirs, enabling a like-for-like comparison.

Our replication confirms their central finding—that recurrent architectures surpass linear and tree-based models on this task—but also reveals a sizeable sensitivity to kernel initialisation.  Table \ref{tab:comparison_lstm_varadharajan} shows that LSTM He initialisation collapses to chance-level accuracy (≈ 0.51), while variance-preserving schemes lift performance into the mid-0.83–0.88 range.  The LeCun Uniform variant achieves a test accuracy of 0.877, marginally higher than Varadharajan’s reported validation score and 1.3 points above their best TF–IDF baseline.  In addition, the relatively small gap between our validation (0.885) and test (0.877) scores for LeCun Uniform suggests better generalisation than that observed in their experiment (train – val gap ≈ 0.036).

These results underscore two practical implications.  First, weight initialisation is a first-order hyper-parameter for LSTMs: swapping He for LeCun changes final accuracy by nearly 37 points.  Second, carefully tuned recurrent networks now close the performance gap with Transformer baselines reported in recent IMDb studies, yet retain lower parameter counts and faster convergence.  For practitioners constrained by compute or latency budgets, a LeCun-or Glorot-initialised LSTM therefore offers an attractive accuracy–efficiency trade-off.

\section*{Conclusion \& Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Limitations and threats to validity}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Single corpus.}  Results are confined to IMDb sentiment.
        Domain-shift experiments (news, tweets, legal text) may alter the
        ranking of initialisers.
  \item \textbf{Fixed optimiser.}  Adam tends to damp exploding gradients;
        SGD with momentum could magnify the LSTM–He failure case.
  \item \textbf{One embedding strategy.}  We used random learnable
        embeddings; incorporating pre-trained GloVe or BERT tokenisers might
        offset some variance effects.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Implications for future work}

Two extensions follow naturally:  
(i) evaluate orthogonal and scaled-orthogonal initialisers that have shown
promise in very deep LSTMs;  
(ii) probe whether Transformer encoders, which remove recurrence but still
stack dozens of non-linear layers, exhibit analogous sensitivity to
variance‐preserving schemes.

Overall, the study highlights that \emph{initialisation remains a
first-order design choice} even after gating mechanisms have mitigated
classic gradient-flow problems.
